The first README for [Galos]({{ '/projects/galos' | relative_url }}) said it
was "somewhere between reality and the flight sim E:D," and then warned that
most of what it described wasn't implemented yet. Both of those stayed true for
a long time. This is the story of how a little program for watching factions
in Elite: Dangerous slowly turned into a map of the whole galaxy, over almost
six years and a lot of time not working on it.

### 2021: Listening to the Galaxy

Elite has a surprisingly open ecosystem. Players run little tools alongside the
game that forward what they see to a shared relay called [EDDN][eddn], and
anyone can subscribe. On February 23rd, 2021 I started Galos by doing exactly
that and printing the feed to my terminal. The next day it was saving systems
to Postgres, then factions and their states, and by the end of the month it was
tracking influence, the numbers that decide who controls a system in the
game's background simulation.

This wasn't just me. Galos lived under a small GitHub organization, ED-NEWP,
and [Yev Krupetsky][yev] pitched in with the CI and a Discord bot. March was the busiest month of the whole first year.
Galos learned to read a commander's own journal files, search for systems by
name, print pretty tables, and estimate fuel costs (until I decided I didn't
trust them and hid them again). There's a commit from that month titled "Can't
use github secrets w/ free plan," which says a lot about the budget.

Then it slowed down. The bot was removed in May. In October I got the code
compiling to WASM and put together a small GUI, and in January 2022 Galos
learned about planets and moons. After that it mostly sat.

### Keeping the Lights On

In 2023 there are a handful of commits, all of them some version of "update deps and
keep the build working." I wasn't building anything new, but I wasn't ready to
let it rot either.

### 2024: A Map

In August 2024 it came back all at once. Galos could import from the EDSM API,
stations showed up with their services and economies, and markets after that.
There's also a sad little commit noting that EDDB, one of the sites we had
pulled data from, had shut down.

Then, in the middle of August, I started on a map. It was written in Rust with
[Bevy][bevy], it loaded systems from the database as the camera moved, and
within a couple of weeks you could search for a system, click on it, and see a
route drawn through the stars. September added a "spyglass" for controlling
how much of the galaxy got fetched at once, along with a lot of refactoring and
at least one commit titled "Throwing shit against a wall here." By October it
was quiet again. In December I added a license, and in April 2025 I made one
small fix so markets would create their stations. That was all of 2025.

### 2026: The Whole Galaxy

This August, Galos woke up harder than it ever had. It started with updating
the map to the latest Bevy, and then kept going for about nine hundred commits
over the next three months.

The biggest change is that the map no longer needs a database. Galos still
subscribes to EDDN and still keeps everything in Postgres, but now it also
builds a spatial index, an octree with its own on-disk format, and the map
draws from that alone. That means it can draw every known system at once, as a
glowing field from far away that resolves into individual stars, names, and
then a system's own planets as you fly in. The planets are drawn where the
game's clock has carried them by now, not where somebody last happened to scan
them.

![The galaxy from above, the core at the top]({{ '/img/galos/galaxy.jpg' | relative_url }})

Then the project grew a second question. Elite's galaxy is a model of the
Milky Way with real catalog stars placed near Sol, so how close is it to the
real thing? Galos now reads the HYG star catalog, works out magnitudes,
temperatures and colors, and renders a patch of sky the way it would actually
look. The Big Dipper became a golden test image. While checking the catalog I
found that throwing away the rows with no measured distance quietly removes
about one naked-eye star in twenty five, and they're almost all distant
supergiants like Mu Cephei. The only reason I know that is because the point
was to compare against reality and not just assume.
